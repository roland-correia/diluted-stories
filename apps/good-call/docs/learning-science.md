# Why Good Call is built the way it is

Good Call is designed around what research says helps people learn and change behaviour, with
attention spans, ADHD and dyslexia in mind. This page lists each design choice, the evidence behind it,
and how strong that evidence is. Where evidence is thin, it says so.

## Short steps, one screen at a time

**What we do.** Each module runs as short steps: an intro with one idea, a few cards to tap through,
the questions one at a time, then a wrap-up. One button at the bottom always says what happens next.

**Why.** Breaking learning into learner-paced segments (the "segmenting principle") improves both
retention and transfer. A meta-analysis of 56 investigations found small-to-medium benefits
(Rey et al., 2019, *Educational Psychology Review*,
[doi:10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9456-4)). Mayer's research on
multimedia learning reports similar gains for segmenting.

**For ADHD.** Delay aversion is a well-described feature of ADHD: waiting for a later, larger reward
is harder. In one study, adding stimulation during the wait reduced the preference of children with
ADHD for small immediate rewards (Antrop et al., 2006, *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry*,
[PubMed 17076754](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17076754/)), though later results are mixed.
Breaking tasks into milestones with feedback along the way is a common accommodation
([Simply Psychology on delay aversion](https://www.simplypsychology.org/?p=82774)). So every step here
ends in a quick win, such as a flipped card, a verdict or points. Evidence for specific app designs
for ADHD is thin, so we have kept to these general principles.

## Answer first, then the reason

**What we do.** Every question asks for a decision before it explains anything, and the feedback
comes straight away, for every option.

**Why.** Retrieval practice (testing yourself) improves long-term retention more than re-reading,
and is strongest when feedback follows (Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan, 2017, *Review of
Educational Research* 87(3)). Feedback that explains why, not just right or wrong, supports transfer
to new situations.

## Little and often: breaks and a daily goal

**What we do.** A break is offered after every five calls. The daily goal is five questions, about
five minutes, shown as a ring on Home. Visitors can plan their next five minutes as a calendar
reminder.

**Why.** Spacing practice over time beats massing it in one sitting, across hundreds of studies
(Cepeda et al., 2006, *Psychological Bulletin* 132). A small, fixed goal is easier to start, which
matters most for people who find starting hard.

## Plan when, not just what: habits

**What we do.** Each module ends with one habit. The visitor picks when they will try it ("Next time
I'm out", or their own words) and taps "I'll try it". On a later visit, Home asks how it went.

**Why.** Implementation intentions ("When X happens, I will do Y") roughly double follow-through
compared with intentions alone: a medium-to-large effect, d = 0.65 across 94 tests (Gollwitzer and
Sheeran, 2006, *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*;
[summary](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes)).
For habits specifically, effects are smaller but still real, d = 0.34 (Adriaanse et al., 2010).

**Missing a day is fine.** In a study of new daily habits, automaticity took 66 days on average, with
a range of 18 to 254, and missing a single day did not undo progress (Lally et al., 2010;
[UCL summary](https://www.ucl.ac.uk/news/2009/aug/how-long-does-it-take-form-habit)). That is why
"Not yet" simply asks again tomorrow, without a broken streak or a telling-off.

## Easy to read

**What we do.** Text is left-aligned by default, in a sans-serif font, at 18px, with short lines.
Justified text, larger text, roomier spacing and softer backgrounds are options under Aa. Every step
can be read aloud.

**Why.** The British Dyslexia Association's style guide recommends left-aligned, unjustified text in
a plain sans-serif font, because justification makes word spacing uneven
([BDA guidance, as reproduced by Boston University](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/jmedday/files/2025/05/Dyslexia-friendly-style-guide.docx);
[Dyslexia Scotland](https://dyslexiascotland.org.uk/dyslexia-friendly-typed-formats)). The Soft and
Cream backgrounds reduce glare for some readers. We make no stronger claim than that: evidence that
any particular colour improves attention is weak.

## Listen as well as read

**What we do.** Every step can be read aloud in a natural recorded voice, warm and upbeat, like a
friend talking you through it. It is never on unless you tap Listen.

**Why.** Hearing text while reading it supports people with dyslexia and other reading difficulties,
and lets anyone learn while their eyes are busy. The evidence is mixed for everyone else: Mayer's
"redundancy principle" finds that narration plus identical on-screen text can slightly reduce
learning for some, because attention is split. So Listen is a choice, never on by default.

## Calm, not compulsive

**What we do.** No timers, no leaderboards, no countdowns, no loss-framed streak warnings. Motion
is gentle and turns off for anyone who asks their device for reduced motion. Points and
achievements celebrate effort and follow-through, not speed.

**Why.** Learning sticks better when people feel safe. Pressure designs raise engagement in the
short term but can undermine motivation and wellbeing, which matters for a platform about consent,
sex and online harm.

## What we have not done

- We have not tested Good Call itself in a trial. These choices follow the evidence above. They
  have not been measured on this app.
- We do not track anyone, so we cannot measure completion or follow-through across visitors. If that
  ever changes, it will be with consent, and listed on the privacy and cookies pages first.
